The Problem

The robot classifies what is inside a sealed cup entirely by touch. Because the lid blocks vision, the arm picks up the cup, swirls it, and identifies the contents by measuring the resulting joint-torque dynamics.

Classifying Without a Force-Torque Sensor

Intuitively, mapping direct force and torque measurements to an object class (liquid, solid, empty) is straightforward. In the absence of an external force-torque sensor, however, we can exploit a latent signal: variations in the payload held by the end-effector induce measurable differences in joint torques throughout a trajectory.

Consider an arm moving in a single dimension: increasing the payload's weight directly increases the torque required to execute that motion. Feels expands on this principle — classifying the hidden contents of a sealed cup strictly through joint-torque dynamics during a fixed motion primitive.

Mapping Swirl Dynamics to a 10-D Signature

Static holds tell us nothing about the contents; the payload signal only appears during motion. To capture it, the robot runs a fixed motion primitive — pick up → swirl → place — and extracts a 10-dimensional feature signature from the torque response.

What Makes Classification Hard

  • Low sampling rate: The arm reports torque telemetry at just 4–5 Hz. High-frequency signals like rattling (5–25 Hz) sit well above the Nyquist limit, forcing the model to rely solely on low-frequency momentum and drag.
  • Baseline drift: Temperature changes and subtle pose shifts cause baseline torques to wander over time. Because static offsets are noisy and unreliable, only dynamic motion yields a clean differential signal.
  • No static payload signal: At this pose, resting hold torques are identical across all contents. Every discriminative cue comes from how the mass shifts during the swirl.
