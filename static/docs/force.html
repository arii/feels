Reading force from joint torques

The textbook relation

Joint torques and end-effector forces are linked through the arm's Jacobian:

τ = Jᵀ(q) · F  +  gravity(q)  +  dynamics(q, q̇, q̈)

A wrist-mounted 6-axis force-torque sensor measures F directly. The same information is recoverable from the arm itself: subtract gravity and the arm's own dynamics, and the leftover torque residuals carry the payload's signature.

What the system does

Full force estimation needs an accurate arm mass model plus a clean tare; the audit measured ~10 N·m of tare drift against a ≤2 N·m payload signal. The pipeline therefore works with residuals — three steps:

  1. Detrend: a low-pass filter removes gravity and slow pose trends, leaving torque residuals — the fast, motion-coupled part of the signal.
  2. Signatures in native units: variance, peak-to-peak swing, and phase lag of the residuals become features. A sloshing liquid shows high residual variance and a measurable phase lag; a rigid weight shows low variance and near-zero lag.
  3. Contrast over calibration: features stay in native units. They only need contrast between classes.

Why this is enough

Classification needs contrast. Water and soap imprint different residuals on the same driven swirl — different viscosity, different slosh. The swirl is the excitation signal; the residuals are the response.

Why joint torques suffice

Every servo arm already streams joint loads — the motor controllers' own telemetry channel. For "which of these is it?", that channel carries enough signal: water and soap imprint different residuals on the same driven swirl.

Measured limits. Residuals are comparable within one fixed macro (pose-dependent gravity is subtracted out). Static offsets carry tare drift, so every feature comes from the motion. Rattle energy is observable only to 4–5 Hz at this telemetry rate.
