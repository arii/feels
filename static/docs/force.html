Effective force measurement without a force-torque sensor

The textbook relation

Joint torques and end-effector forces are linked through the arm's Jacobian:

τ = Jᵀ(q) · F  +  gravity(q)  +  dynamics(q, q̇, q̈)

A wrist-mounted 6-axis force-torque sensor measures F directly. Without one: subtract gravity and the arm's own dynamics, and the leftover torque residuals are the payload.

What we actually do

We do not invert the Jacobian, and we do not output calibrated newtons. Full force estimation would need an accurate mass model of the arm plus a clean tare — and our audit showed the tare baseline is the noisiest part of the system (~10 N·m of drift against a ≤2 N·m payload signal). Instead:

  1. Detrend: a low-pass filter removes gravity and slow pose trends, leaving torque residuals — the fast, motion-coupled part of the signal.
  2. Signature, not newtons: variance, peak-to-peak swing, and phase lag of the residuals become features. A sloshing liquid shows high residual variance and a measurable phase lag; a rigid weight shows low variance and near-zero lag.
  3. Compare, don't calibrate: features are never converted to force units. They only need contrast between classes.

Why this is enough

Classification needs contrast, not newtons. Water and soap imprint different residuals on the same driven swirl — different viscosity, different slosh. The swirl is the excitation signal; the residuals are the response.

Why not just mount the sensor?

A 6-axis F/T sensor costs $5k+, needs wrist integration and calibration. Every servo arm already reports joint loads — the motor controllers' own telemetry channel. For "which of these is it?" (not "how many newtons?"), that channel carries enough signal.

What we don't claim. No calibrated force output. Pose-dependent gravity terms are removed, not modeled — so residuals are only comparable within one fixed macro. And tare drift means static offsets are untrustworthy; everything we use comes from the motion.
